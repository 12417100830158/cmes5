// About this tool

Created by: Mai — Computational Social Science, University of Amsterdam, 2026

Quantum computing utilises quantum mechanics to solve complex problems faster than even the most powerful classical computers. While emerging as a promising technology for scientific breakthroughs, it brings forward ethical concerns. There is a possibility that quantum computing can break through current encryption algorithms: “Most of our digital infrastructure, and basically anything we do online—whether it is video conferencing, sending e-mails, or accessing our online bank account—is encrypted through cryptographic protocols based on the difficulty of solving factorization problems (i.e., the RSA algorithm)” (Possati, 2023). These factorisation problems and algorithms that encryptions rely on are currently secure as it takes thousands of years to solve the problem; however, with quantum computing, the time to solve find these factors is completely reduced, making it possible to decrypt any communication and code and posing serious security concern: “Privacy and intelligence will be seriously damaged, and governments will face major problems as they try to defend their communications” (Possati, 2023).

The dilemma: Many entities, including intelligence agencies, hackers, and other institutions, are currently collecting encrypted civilian data, intending to decrypt it retroactively once quantum computers become available (Cooper et al., 2024; Jena, 2025; Kagai et al., 2025; Mascelli & Rodden, 2023). This practice raises ethical questions of consent, as citizens have not agreed to such data collection and may be unaware that it is occurring. This tool applies Rawls's veil of ignorance: choose the rules before you know your role.

Purpose: This tool aims to inform people about quantum computing and the potential threat to data privacy and cybersecurity as a result of the technology. Hopefully, it can provide some information and insights for users to engage in discussions about this emerging problem and possible solutions to advocate for.

Frameworks applied: Justice as fairness (Rawls), procedural ethics, ethics of care.

Data shown is illustrative — generated to communicate the scale of the ethical problem. The program it describes is real.

// The scenario — 2026
A government program has been collecting encrypted civilian data since 2020. In approximately 8 years, a quantum computer will decrypt everything stored.

You are designing the policy. You do not yet know whether you will be the intelligence director or one of the 280 million people in the database.

Classical computers process information as bits of either 0 or 1. Quantum computers use qubits, which can exist in multiple states simultaneously through a property called superposition. Combined with entanglement, where qubits become linked so the state of one instantly influences another, quantum computers can evaluate enormous numbers of possibilities at once. For certain problems, including breaking encryption, this makes them exponentially faster than anything that exists today.
